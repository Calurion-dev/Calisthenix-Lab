Methods · Cross-cutting level

Nine ways to train. What each one is for.

A method is not a religion: it is a tool. The five fundamental movements do not change; what changes is how you organise them, load them and spread them across the week.

Choosing a method means answering three questions: what is your goal, how much time do you have, and what is your current level. A beginner does not need EMOM or street lifting; they need clean reps and consistency. An advanced practitioner no longer needs the push-up explained: they need to structure their week.

The nine methods below do not exclude each other. Most practitioners combine two or three: classic sets for the base, a little heavy strength work, and a circuit or an EMOM now and then for conditioning. What matters is knowing why you are using a tool — and when to stop.

01

Circuits

Conditioning

In short. Link several exercises with little or no rest, then repeat the whole thing. One round = one sequence of exercises; usually 3 to 5 rounds.

Who it is for. Saving time, building conditioning, varying without equipment. It is also a good way to discover several movements in one session.

Careful. Fatigue arrives fast and technique degrades first. Put technical movements at the start of the circuit, never at the end.

Benchmarks4–6 exercises · 8–12 reps · 30–90 s between rounds

02

Reps & sets

Base

In short. The base method, from which all the others derive: a number of sets, a number of reps, a defined rest between sets.

Who it is for. Building a measurable base and progressing without getting lost. It is the simplest format to follow and adjust.

Careful. Write your sets down. Without a written reference, you cannot tell whether you are progressing — and you compare sessions that have nothing in common.

Benchmarks3–5 sets · 5–12 reps · 60–180 s rest depending on the goal

03

GTG (Grease the Groove)

Technique

In short. Repeat a submaximal movement several times a day, with plenty of rest, never going to failure.

Who it is for. Unlocking a specific movement — pull-ups, push-ups — and improving technique through quality repetition.

Careful. This is not a hypertrophy programme. Warm up, stay far from failure, and do not stack it with intense sessions of the same movement.

Benchmarks3–6 sets at 40–60 % of max, spread through the day · 5–6 days/week

04

Push / pull

Organisation

In short. Organise sessions by pattern — a pushing day, a pulling day — rather than working everything every time.

Who it is for. Increasing volume per muscle group while still recovering. Useful when full-body sessions become too long.

Careful. Balance is the key. The back and pulling work are often the poor relations: count your sets on both sides.

Benchmarks2–4 sessions/week · alternate push / pull · 48 h between two sessions of the same pattern

05

Strength / street lifting

Strength

In short. Add load — weight belt, vest — to the fundamental movements, with few reps and long rests. Street lifting is the competitive version of this approach.

Who it is for. Developing maximal strength and density, not volume. Reserve it for movements you already own at bodyweight.

Careful. Technique comes before load. One slow, clean rep is worth more than a record yanked up with your back.

Benchmarks3–5 sets · 3–6 reps · 2–4 min rest · 1–2 times/week per movement

06

Muscular endurance

Endurance

In short. Train your ability to sustain effort: long sets, high reps, short rests. Your body learns to last, not only to produce a peak of strength.

Who it is for. Pull-ups, push-ups, planks: holding longer and recovering better between sets.

Careful. Range of motion must not shrink with fatigue. If it does, the set is over — not the movement.

Benchmarks2–4 sets · 15–30+ reps (or 40–60 s plank) · 30–60 s rest

07

Freestyle / combos

Technique

In short. Link skills and transitions with fluidity, often on a bar: the expressive and creative side of the discipline.

Who it is for. Practitioners comfortable with the basics, who want to create, control and give their movement some style.

Careful. Risk of falling and of the bar. Never a new combo alone, never without a warm-up, never on a doubtful support.

BenchmarksShort sessions · 2–3 movements per combo · full warm-up first

08

EMOM

Conditioning

In short. Every Minute On the Minute: a set at the start of each minute, the rest of the minute is your rest. The structure is simple, the fatigue is real.

Who it is for. Density and conditioning, with a format that is easy to follow without fancy equipment.

Careful. If you cannot finish the set within the minute, reduce the reps. The minute is not a goal: it is a timer.

Benchmarks8–15 min · 3–8 reps per minute · rest = the remaining time

09

Pyramid

Volume

In short. Vary the number of reps — or the load — from set to set: 1-2-3-4-3-2-1, for example.

Who it is for. Building volume without monotony and managing fatigue across a session. The climb prepares, the descent confirms.

Careful. Total volume matters more than the top of the pyramid. Do not turn the last set into a test to failure.

Benchmarks6–10 sets · climb then descend · rest adapted to the difficulty

Choosing

Which method for which goal?

Build volume

Reps & sets, pyramid, push / pull. Measurable sets and a total volume that is easy to track.

Get stronger

Strength / street lifting, GTG, reps & sets. Few reps, plenty of quality and rest.

Last longer

Muscular endurance, circuits, EMOM. Your body learns to repeat effort and recover quickly.

Create and link

Freestyle, GTG, push / pull. Technique first, expression second.

No method replaces techniqueIf a method pushes you to sacrifice range of motion, change the method — not the execution. The benchmarks above are general and do not replace professional advice.
